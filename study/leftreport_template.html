The
McGregor left

His straight left is the punch people remember: the pull counter on Aldo, the counters on Mendes and Alvarez. We found every one we could in fifteen fights, frame by frame at the broadcast's own rate, and set each against his opponents' rear straight thrown in the same fights.

What made it his isn't how it moved. It's how often he threw it. Measured from the body, his left is an ordinary fast rear straight: the same speed, reach and footwork as the rear hands thrown back at him. But he threw it about {{rate_ratio}} times as often as his opponents threw theirs, in {{rate_n}} fights. And he threw it without a jab: a lead-hand set-up came before it half as often as before theirs.

Before the left · the head and the torso

Slip and rip, or stand and fire?

The 0.8 s before each straight left, his head measured against his own hips, so his footwork and the camera's pan drop out. A broadcast camera sees a pull (head back) and a dip (head down) clearly; a slip toward or away from the camera it can't, so these are lower bounds.

Before most lefts he moved his head no more than at any other moment. His opponents moved theirs twice as often before throwing. Most of the time the left came from a still head, set off by timing and distance, not a slip. When he did move first, the move was real and it came in two distinct shapes.

{{head_paths}}
pull counterslip counterother lefts

The pull counter is two beats. The head goes back, away from the punch, about 0.17 torso lengths, with little drop. The left follows about 170 ms after the head reaches its furthest point: away, then back in.

The slip counter is one motion. The head drops about 0.16 torso lengths, with little backward travel, and the left is already going at the bottom of the dip: the punch and the slip are the same movement.

Over time a growing share of his lefts came off some head or torso movement (ρ +0.57, p 0.08: suggestive), and the moves got slightly bigger after 2016.

The lead hand

Why not behind the jab?

His opponents threw their rear straight behind the lead hand twice as often as he did. The obvious guess was that he held his lead hand out still, as a range-finder, and fired past it. The data says no: in the second before a left, his lead hand was never held out and still. It was busy, but it wasn't jabbing.

The lead hand moved, pawed, touched and measured, but it rarely snapped out as a punch. The left wasn't the second half of a one-two; it was the first punch, timed off the opponent rather than set up by his own jab. That is also why "straight lead" and "caught coming in" are his two most common set-ups.

The rear hand was his main hand all along: about half his punches, against roughly a third for the men he fought, higher in 10 of 11 fights. The left hook faded (from about 2.3 to 0.9 a minute, ρ −0.56, p 0.08) while the straight left held steady near 3.7 a minute: over the years the left got straighter. Total volume, jabs and kicks did not change in a way the opponents didn't share.

What came before it · exploratory

How he set it up

Each punch is sorted by what happened in the 0.6 s before it, by fixed rules. Counters come within 0.7 s of an opponent attack: a pull counter if his head first moved ≥ 0.15 torso lengths away, a slip counter if it dropped ≥ 0.12, otherwise head still. Caught coming in: the opponent was advancing. Behind the paw: his lead hand had reached out first. Straight lead: none of these.

The clearest difference: his opponents threw their rear straight behind the lead hand (the one-two) 37 % of the time; McGregor 18 %. He threw it cold, as a lead, or into a man walking forward, about twice as often as they did.

The famous pull counter is real but rare: 9 % of his lefts (7 punches), against 5 % of theirs. All counters together: 36 % of his lefts, 31 % of theirs. Counters became a larger share of his lefts over his career (ρ {{counter_rho}}, p {{counter_p}}: suggestive only). By fight: {{counter_points}}.

Limits

What could fool this

  • Detection. A punch is a fast extension of the arm toward the opponent (opens ≥ 0.2 of its length within 400 ms to ≥ 0.65, wrist ≥ 8 torso lengths/s, ≥ 0.3 torso lengths forward). Checked by eye on random samples: about three in four detections are committed left hands; the rest are feints, clinch hand-fighting or unclear angles. Straight vs hook (from the wrist's path) is right more often than not, not always.
  • Recall. A punch thrown away from the camera or behind the opponent's body can't be seen. Rates are lower bounds, and the comparison with opponents shares the same blind spots.
  • Landed vs missed. The video can't say. The opponent's head moving away in the next 250 ms was measured and differed little by set-up; it's noisy and isn't proof of contact.
  • Small numbers. {{n_lefts}} straight lefts (plus {{n_hooks}} hook-shaped ones) and {{n_opp}} opponent rear straights in fifteen fights. Set-up shares carry wide intervals.
  • Fixed after looking. The detector was tuned by eye on the Khabib fight before any other fight was run, and a frame-rate bug (the 25 fps Siver upload) was fixed. Both are logged in the pre-registration file.
